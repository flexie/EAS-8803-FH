Part I: Motivation

Why Uncertainty Quantification?

  • Seismic inversion produces a single velocity model — but how confident should we be?
  • Subsurface characterization is fundamentally ill-posed: many velocity models can explain the observed data equally well (Tarantola, 2005)
  • Decision-making (e.g., well placement, reservoir management) requires not just a best estimate but a distribution of plausible models consistent with the data
  • Traditional full-waveform inversion (FWI) gives a point estimate — no uncertainty information (Virieux and Operto, 2009)

Motivate the need for probabilistic approaches to subsurface imaging.

The Challenge of Bayesian Inference for FWI

  • Bayesian inference provides a principled framework for UQ
  • But the posterior \(p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\) is intractable for realistic problems
  • MCMC methods require \(\mathcal{O}(10^5\text{--}10^6)\) forward PDE solves — each costs minutes to hours in 3D
  • This makes MCMC computationally infeasible for production-scale FWI
  • We need scalable approaches that combine physics with modern machine learning

Our Approach: Generative AI + Wave Physics

  • Combine conditional generative models (normalizing flows, diffusion networks) with wave physics
  • Key innovation: use physics-based summary statistics to make the problem tractable
  • Develop a progression of methods:
    • WISE: Amortized inference with subsurface extensions (Yin et al., 2024b)
    • ASPIRE: Iterative refinement of amortized inference (Orozco et al., 2025)
    • WISER: Semi-amortized inference closing the amortization gap (Yin et al., 2024a)
    • ML-enabled velocity building: Scalable deployment with quantitative UQ (Orozco et al., 2024)

Part II: Background — Bayesian Inference

Conditional Distributions

  • In many problems, we want to infer an unknown quantity \(\mathbf{x}\) given observations \(\mathbf{y}\)
  • The conditional distribution \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\) encodes our knowledge about \(\mathbf{x}\) after seeing data \(\mathbf{y}\)
  • Example: given seismic shot records \(\mathbf{y}\), what are the plausible velocity models \(\mathbf{x}\)?
  • The conditional distribution captures all the information about \(\mathbf{x}\) that is contained in \(\mathbf{y}\)

Bayes’ Theorem

\[ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\, p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{y})} \]

Term Name Meaning
\(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\) Posterior Distribution of \(\mathbf{x}\) given data \(\mathbf{y}\)
\(p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\) Likelihood How well model \(\mathbf{x}\) explains data \(\mathbf{y}\)
\(p(\mathbf{x})\) Prior Our beliefs about \(\mathbf{x}\) before seeing data
\(p(\mathbf{y})\) Evidence Normalizing constant (intractable!)

Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)

  • Classical Bayesian inference requires evaluating the likelihood \(p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\) analytically
  • In many scientific problems, we have a simulator \(\mathcal{F}\) (e.g., wave equation solver) but no closed-form likelihood
  • Simulation-based inference (Cranmer et al., 2020) learns the posterior directly from simulated pairs \(\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \mathbf{y}^{(i)})\}\)
    • Sample from prior: \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)} \sim p(\mathbf{x})\)
    • Simulate: \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\)
    • Train a neural network to approximate \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\)

Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)

  • UQ goes beyond point estimates to characterize the range of plausible solutions
  • Key quantities:
    • Posterior mean: \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}]\) — a good single estimate
    • Posterior variance: \(\text{Var}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}]\) — how uncertain we are at each point
    • Posterior samples: \(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}^{(M)} \sim p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\) — individual plausible models
  • Enables downstream uncertainty propagation (e.g., uncertainty in migrated images or ML-based image segmentation)

Summary Statistics

  • Raw seismic data \(\mathbf{y}\) is very high-dimensional (millions of samples)
  • A summary statistic \(h(\mathbf{y})\) compresses data into a lower-dimensional, informative representation
  • Good summary statistics are sufficient: \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{x}\mid h(\mathbf{y}))\)
  • Examples:
    • Reverse-time migration (RTM): \(h(\mathbf{y}) = \nabla\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y}\)
    • Common-image gathers (CIGs): \(h(\mathbf{y}) = \overline{\nabla\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y}\)
  • Physics-based summaries leverage knowledge of the forward operator

Normalizing Flows — Unconditional

  • A normalizing flow is an invertible neural network \(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) that transforms a simple distribution (e.g., Gaussian) into a complex one
  • Forward: \(\mathbf{z} = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\) maps data \(\mathbf{x}\) to latent \(\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\)
  • Inverse: \(\mathbf{x} = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\mathbf{z})\) generates samples from noise
  • Training: maximize likelihood using the change-of-variables formula

\[ \log p(\mathbf{x}) = \log p(\mathbf{z}) + \log\left|\det \mathbf{J}_{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right| \]

Normalizing Flows — Conditional

  • Conditional normalizing flows (CNFs) learn \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\) by conditioning on observed data
  • The network \(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{y})\) transforms velocity model \(\mathbf{x}\) to noise \(\mathbf{z}\), conditioned on data \(\mathbf{y}\)
  • After training, draw samples: \(\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), then \(\mathbf{x} = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}; \mathbf{y})\)
  • Each sample \(\mathbf{x}\) is a plausible velocity model consistent with \(\mathbf{y}\)

The Forward Problem

The seismic forward problem (Orozco et al., 2024):

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{y}\): observed shot records (seismic data)
  • \(\mathbf{x}\): subsurface velocity model (what we want to recover)
  • \(\mathcal{F}\): forward operator (solution of the wave equation PDE restricted to receivers)
  • \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\): noise

Likelihood: \(p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2\right)\)

Posterior Samples via CNFs

  • Given a trained CNF \(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}\) and observed data \(\mathbf{y}_\text{obs}\):
    1. Compute summary statistic: \(\overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs} = h(\mathbf{y}_\text{obs})\)
    2. Draw random noise: \(\mathbf{z}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), for \(i = 1, \ldots, M\)
    3. Generate posterior samples: \(\mathbf{x}_i = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_i; \overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs})\)
  • Conditional mean: \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\text{CM} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \mathbf{x}_i\)
  • Conditional standard deviation: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{i=1}^M (\mathbf{x}_i - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\text{CM})^2}\)

Amortized vs. Non-Amortized Inference

Amortized inference:

  • Train once on many simulated pairs \(\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \mathbf{y}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N\)
  • At inference: just pass new \(\mathbf{y}_\text{obs}\) through trained network — very fast!
  • \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)})\|_2^2 - \log|\det\mathbf{J}_{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|\right)\)
  • But: generalizing across datasets introduces an amortization gap

Non-amortized inference:

  • Optimize for each specific \(\mathbf{y}_\text{obs}\) individually
  • Better approximation but much more expensive (many PDE solves per dataset)

Part III: WISE

WISE: Training Objective

WISE = full-Waveform variational Inference via Subsurface Extensions (Yin et al., 2024b)

CNF training objective (Eq. 1 from Yin et al. (2024b)):

\[ \underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{minimize}} \quad \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\frac{1}{2}\|f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\right)\|_2^2-\log\left|\det\mathbf{J}_{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right|\right) \]

  • \(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\): conditional normalizing flow with parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\)
  • \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\): velocity model sample from the prior
  • \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\): corresponding observation (summary statistic)
  • \(\mathbf{J}_{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\): Jacobian of the flow
  • \(\|\cdot\|_2^2\): enforces that transformed samples follow a standard Gaussian
  • \(\log|\det\mathbf{J}|\): volume-preservation term

Physics-Based Summary Statistics

Why not use raw data? Training CNFs on raw seismic data is impractical.

RTM as summary statistic (Siahkoohi et al., 2023):

\[h_\text{RTM}(\mathbf{y}) = \nabla\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y}\]

  • Problem: for inaccurate background-velocity model, \(\mathbf{x}_0\), RTM does not correctly summarize the data
  • \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}) \neq p(\mathbf{x}\mid\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y})\) when \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is inaccurate

CIGs as summary statistic (WISE innovation):

\[h_\text{CIG}(\mathbf{y}) = \overline{\nabla\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y}\]

  • CIGs from extended Born modeling are closer to an isometry
  • \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}) \approx p(\mathbf{x}\mid\overline{\nabla\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y})\) — even for inaccurate \(\mathbf{x}_0\)!

WISE — Conclusions

  • CIGs as summary statistics preserve more information than RTM, especially for inaccurate migration velocities
  • WISE produces distributions of velocity models conditioned on seismic data in ~6 minutes per inference
  • Enables quantification of amplitude and positioning uncertainty in migration
  • Limitation: the amortization gap — CNFs optimize across many datasets rather than excelling at any single observation
  • Computational cost: ~80 hours for training pairs, ~16 hours for CNF training, but amortized across all future inferences

Part IV: ASPIRE

ASPIRE: Closing the Amortization Gap

ASPIRE = Amortized Posterior Inference with Refined Estimates (Orozco et al., 2025)

Key insight: Iteratively refine the physics-based summary statistics to extract more information from the data

The gradient of the log-likelihood is a maximally informative summary statistic:

\[\overline{\mathbf{y}} = \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\big|_{\mathbf{x}_0} = \nabla\mathcal{F}^\top[\mathbf{x}_0](\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_0) - \mathbf{y})\]

Quality depends on the fiducial point=background velocity \(\mathbf{x}_0\) being close to the true model. ASPIRE uses posterior means as improved fiducials!

ASPIRE — Conclusions

  • Iteratively refines summary statistics by moving fiducial points closer to maximum likelihood estimates
  • Each iteration extracts more information from the data while remaining amortized
  • Online cost: only ~8 PDE solves vs. \(10^4\)+ for non-amortized methods
  • Achieves quality close to the non-amortized gold standard at a fraction of the cost
  • Combines generative modeling, physics-hybrid methods, and statistics in a principled framework

Part V: WISER

Out-of-Distribution and Model Misspecification

Challenge: Amortized methods are trained on synthetic data from a specific prior

  • At deployment, real data may be out-of-distribution (OOD): geology, noise levels, or acquisition parameters differ from training
  • Model misspecification: the simulator (e.g., acoustic) may not match reality (e.g., elastic)
  • WISE’s posterior samples under OOD conditions closely resemble training-distribution velocities — inductive bias from training

WISER addresses this by adding physics-based refinements on top of amortized inference (Yin et al., 2024a)

WISER Equation 1 — Strong Formulation

The objective for physics-based refinements (Eq. 1, Yin et al. (2024a)):

\[ \underset{\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\operatorname{minimize}} \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})} \; \mathbb{KL}\!\left(p(h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z})) \,\|\, p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}(\mathbf{z}\mid\overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs})\right) \]

Expanded:

\[ = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathcal{F}\circ f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}^{-1}(h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z});\overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs}) - \mathbf{y}_\text{obs}\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{2}\|h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z})\|_2^2 - \log|\det\mathbf{J}_{h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}| \]

Term Role
Data misfit Ties posterior samples to observed data
\(\frac{1}{2}\|h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z})\|_2^2\) Keeps corrected latent space near Gaussian (prior)
\(-\log\|\det\mathbf{J}_{h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\|\) Prevents mode collapse

WISER — Weak Formulation

Computationally efficient weak formulation (Eq. 2, Yin et al. (2024a)):

\[ \underset{\mathbf{x}_{1:M},\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\operatorname{minimize}} \; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_i)-\mathbf{y}_\text{obs}\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\|\mathbf{x}_i - f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}^{-1}(h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_i);\overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs})\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{2}\|h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_i)\|_2^2 - \log|\det\mathbf{J}_{h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}|\right] \]

Key components:

  • \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^M\): particles (velocity model samples) updated via gradient descent with wave physics
  • \(h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\): shallow refinement network correcting the latent space
  • \(\gamma\): trade-off parameter (\(\gamma=0\) recovers the strong form)
  • Key insight: decouples expensive forward operator \(\mathcal{F}\) from cheaper network evaluations

WISER Algorithm 1 — Offline Phase

Dataset generation (same as WISE):

  1. For \(i = 1, \ldots, N\):
    • Draw \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)} \sim p(\mathbf{x})\), \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)} \sim p(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\)
    • Simulate: \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\)
    • Compute CIGs: \(\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)} = \overline{\nabla\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top \mathbf{y}^{(i)}\)

Train CNF (same as WISE):

\[\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)})\|_2^2 - \log|\det\mathbf{J}_{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|\right)\]

WISER Algorithm 1 — Online Phase

Amortized initialization (fast):

  • Compute CIGs: \(\overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs} = \overline{\nabla\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_0)^\top\mathbf{y}_\text{obs}\)
  • Generate initial samples: \(\mathbf{x}_i = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_i; \overline{\mathbf{y}}_\text{obs})\) for \(\mathbf{z}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\)

Physics-based refinements (alternating optimization):

  • Outer loop: update particles \(\mathbf{x}_i\) via gradient descent with wave physics (costly)
  • Inner loop: update refinement network \(h_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\) with ADAM — no PDE solves needed (cheap)

The outer loop performs FWI-like updates; the inner loop keeps the generative model consistent.

WISER — Case Study

  • 2D slices of the Compass model with acoustic wave physics
  • 512 x 256 gridpoints, 12.5 m resolution (\(>10^5\) degrees of freedom)
  • OOD scenario: elementwise velocity perturbation + higher noise (S/N = 0 dB)
  • WISER parameters:
    • 160 outer iterations (gradient descent with wave physics)
    • 16 particles
    • 1 OBN per gradient (stochastic)
    • 128 inner iterations (network update, no PDE solves)
    • Frequency continuation from low to high
  • Total cost: ~40 data passes (5120 PDE solves) — far fewer than non-amortized VI

WISER — Conclusions

  • Semi-amortized framework: combines fast amortized initialization with physics-based refinements
  • Uses WISE as a conditional prior (conditioned on observed data) — more informative than unconditional priors
  • Closes the amortization gap, especially for out-of-distribution scenarios
  • Computationally feasible: outer loop is costly but inner loop is cheap
  • NFs’ invertible layers enable memory-efficient backpropagation (constant memory regardless of depth)
  • Sets the stage for deploying generative AI to facilitate high-dimensional Bayesian inference in 3D

Quantitative Assessment of UQ

Four metrics to assess UQ quality (Orozco et al., 2024):

  1. z-score — percentage of pixels where error \(>\) 2\(\times\) uncertainty. Lower is better.
  2. Uncertainty Calibration Error (UCE) — correlation between predicted uncertainty and actual error. Lower is better.
  3. Posterior coverage — fraction of pixels where the ground truth falls within the 1st–99th percentile of posterior samples. Higher is better.
  4. Data fit — ratio \(\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\|_2 / \|\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_\text{post}) - \mathbf{y}^\text{obs}\|_2\) as percentage; 100% = perfect fit. Importantly, does not require ground truth.

Conclusions

  • Conditional diffusion networks advance the state of the art in amortized migration-velocity building with UQ
  • CIGs as summary statistics improve all UQ metrics across datasets
  • Four proposed metrics provide a systematic benchmark for UQ assessment
  • Iterative ASPIRE workflow with salt flooding handles complex salt scenarios
  • Posterior velocity models propagate uncertainty to final RTM images
  • Computationally feasible: fast inference with manageable offline training costs
  • First demonstration of ML-enabled probabilistic velocity building from short-offset acoustic reflection-only data

Outlook

  • Representative training datasets remain the key challenge for amortized methods
  • Path forward: curate datasets for a foundation model using field data + well-log pairs from national repositories
  • Non-amortized refinements (WISER, ASPIRE) are crucial for bridging the gap between synthetic training and real-world deployment
  • Field data application revealed bias toward synthetic training distribution — highlights the need for realistic training data
  • Extension to 3D is computationally viable thanks to memory-efficient invertible architectures

Summary of Outcomes

  • Physics-based summary statistics (CIGs) are key to reliable amortized inference
  • Amortized methods (WISE, diffusion networks) provide fast, cost-effective first approximations
  • Iterative refinement (ASPIRE) progressively improves results by extracting more information from data
  • Semi-amortized methods (WISER) close the amortization gap using physics-based fine-tuning
  • Quantitative UQ metrics (z-score, UCE, coverage, data fit) enable systematic evaluation
  • Downstream uncertainty propagation to migrated images is now computationally feasible

  • Inverse problems are central to Earth sciences — from seismic imaging to reservoir characterization
  • Bayesian inference provides the principled framework for quantifying uncertainty
  • Machine learning makes large-scale Bayesian inference computationally feasible
  • Key concepts covered:
    • Forward and inverse problems
    • Bayesian inference and posterior sampling
    • Normalizing flows and diffusion models
    • Physics-informed summary statistics
    • Amortized vs. non-amortized inference
    • Quantitative UQ assessment

Outlook

  • Foundation models for geophysics: train once, deploy everywhere
  • 3D deployment: memory-efficient architectures make this possible
  • Multi-physics: extend beyond acoustic to elastic, anisotropic models
  • Real-world deployment: bridge the simulation gap with semi-amortized methods
  • Beyond velocity: apply to other geophysical parameters (anisotropy, attenuation)
  • Decision support: propagate uncertainty to reservoir management and risk assessment
  • The future of geophysical imaging is probabilistic — not deterministic!
