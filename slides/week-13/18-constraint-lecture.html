This is the second lecture in a series on FWI extensions. We build on the WRI foundations from the previous lecture and introduce constrained optimization for FWI/WRI, focusing on edge-preserving regularization for salt imaging.

Motivation

Avoiding local minima

Full-waveform inversion solves a non-linear least-squares problem (Esser et al. 2018):

\[ \min_{m} \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_s}\|F(m)q_j - d_j\|_2^2 \]

where \(F(m) = PA(m)^{-1}\) is the forward operator combining the PDE solve \(A(m)^{-1}\) with measurement \(P\).

Problem: The objective has parasitic local minima due to cycle-skipping.

Consider,

\[ F(c)q = \left( \begin{matrix} \mathcal{F}^{-1}e^{\imath\omega\|x_1 - x_s\|_2/c}\mathcal{F}q\\ \mathcal{F}^{-1}e^{\imath\omega\|x_2 - x_s\|_2/c}\mathcal{F}q\\ \mathcal{F}^{-1}e^{\imath\omega\|x_3 - x_s\|_2/c}\mathcal{F}q\\ \end{matrix} \right), \] where \(\mathcal{F}\) denotes the temporal Fourier transform, \(\omega\) the angular frequency, \(\imath=\sqrt{-1}\), \(x_i\) is the receiver location, \(x_s\) is the source location, and \(c\) is the soundspeed of the medium.

Avoiding local minima

Extended formulation (WRI) mitigates this by relaxing the PDE constraint (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2013).

This takes folllowing form for above problem:

\[ \min_{m,\Delta q} \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_s}\|F(m)(q_j + \Delta q_j) - d_j\|_2^2 + \lambda^2 \|\Delta q_j\|_2^2 \]

As \(\lambda \uparrow \infty\), \(\|\Delta q\|_2\downarrow 0\) and we recover the original problem.

Example 1 — Local minima in FWI

  • Conventional (\(\lambda\rightarrow\infty\)): multiple local minima clearly visible
  • Extended (\(\lambda=0.1\)): local minima are effectively mitigated
  • Band-limited data makes both large- and small-scale recovery difficult

Adapted from Esser et al. (2018), Fig. 1.

Example 2 — Why constraints matter

Recover 1D velocity \(c(z)\) from band-pass filtered data \(d = F(c)q\):

\[ \min_{c\geq c_0} \|F(c)q - d\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to}\quad Dc\geq 0 \]

  • Bounds (\(c \geq c_0 = 1500\,\mathrm{m/s}\)) + monotonicity (\(Dc \geq 0\)) recover broadband velocity from bandlimited data

Adapted from Esser et al. (2018), Fig. 2.

Tikhonov regularization

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m}} f(\mathbf{m}) + \frac{\alpha}{2}\|R_1\mathbf{m}\|^2+ \frac{\beta}{2}\|R_2\mathbf{m}\|^2 \]

  • well-known & successful technique
  • is differentiable
  • not an exact penalty
  • regularization may adversely affect gradient & Hessian
  • requires non-trivial choices for hyper parameters
  • not easily extended to edge-preserving norms
  • no guarantees that all model iterates are regularized

Regularization w/ constraints

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m}} f(\mathbf{m})\quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{m}\in \mathcal{C}_1\cap\mathcal{C}_2 \]

  • not well-known in our community
  • requires understanding of latest optimization techniques
  • does not affect gradient & Hessian
  • easier parameterization
  • able to uniquely project onto intersection of multiple constraint sets
  • constraints do not need to be differentiable
  • constraints are satisfied at every model iterate

Proxy projection

Find model \(\mathbf{m}\), closest to \(\mathbf{x}\), such that it satisfies the constraints:

\[ \mathcal{P}_\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})=\arg\min_\mathbf{m}\|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{x}\|_2^2\quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{m}\in \mathcal{C}_1\cap\mathcal{C}_2 \]

yielding

  • nonlinear “minimal complexity” best approximation of \(\mathbf{m}\)
  • \(\mathbf{m}\rightarrow \mathbf{x}\) when \(\tau\rightarrow\tau_0=\mathrm{TV}(\mathbf{x})\) and \(\mathbf{m}\in \mathrm{Box}\)
  • edge preserving

Part I: Penalties vs. Constraints — Practical Aspects

Based on (Peters and Herrmann 2017) and (Esser et al. 2016)

Total-variation norm — velocity blocking

The total-variation (TV) norm of a velocity model \(\mathbf{m}\) (Rudin, Osher, and Fatemi 1992):

\[ \text{TV}(\mathbf{m}) = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{ij}\sqrt{({m}_{i+1,j}-{m}_{i,j})^2 + ({m}_{i,j+1}-m_{i,j})^2} \]

Combined with box constraints (\(l \leq m_{i,j} \leq u\)), the projection:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m}}\|\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{m}_\ast\|^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{m}\in \text{Box} \text{ and } \text{TV}(\mathbf{m}) \leq \tau \]

finds a unique blocked velocity model whose blockiness depends on \(\tau\).

Peters and Herrmann (2017)

Projecting onto TV + Box constraints

  • As \(\tau\) increases, the projected model is less blocky and closer to the original
  • The parameter \(\tau\) predictably controls model complexity

Peters and Herrmann (2017), Fig. 1.

FWI with TV-like penalties

\[\min_{\mathbf{m}} f(\mathbf{m})+\alpha \text{TV}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{m})\]

  • Smooth approximation: \(\text{TV}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{m}) = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{ij}\sqrt{(m_{i+1,j}-m_{i,j})^2 + (m_{i,j+1}-m_{i,j})^2 + \epsilon^2}\)
  • No distinctive progression from “blocked” to less blocky
  • Unpredictable behavior as a function of \(\alpha\) and \(\epsilon\)

Peters and Herrmann (2017), Fig. 2.

FWI with TV constraints — predictable

  • Predictable progression from blocky to detailed as \(\tau\) increases
  • Only one parameter (\(\tau\)) to set — directly measurable from initial model
  • Model iterates always satisfy constraints

Peters and Herrmann (2017), Fig. 3.

Why constraints?

  1. Constraints translate prior information more directly than penalties
    • \(\tau\) is directly measurable from a starting model; trade-off parameter \(\alpha\) is not
  1. Absence of user-specified weights
    • Unique projections via Dykstra’s algorithm (Dykstra 1983) — no weight selection needed
    • Constraints do not alter the objective (data misfit)
  1. Constraints are only activated when necessary
    • If a proposed model satisfies all constraints, the projection does nothing
    • Data-fitting and constraint handling are uncoupled
  1. Constraints are satisfied at each iteration
    • Penalty methods do not guarantee this — making them prone to local minima

Peters and Herrmann (2017)

Algorithm — proj. gradient w/ Dykstra

Pseudo-code combining projected gradients with Dykstra’s algorithm (Dykstra 1983) for intersection of constraints.
Initialize: define constraint sets (bounds, TV, others)
For \(k = 1, 2, \ldots\)
\(\quad\) 1. Compute gradient \(\nabla_\mathbf{m} f(\mathbf{m}_k)\)
\(\quad\) 2. Gradient step: \(\tilde{\mathbf{m}} = \mathbf{m}_k - \gamma\nabla_\mathbf{m} f(\mathbf{m}_k)\)
\(\quad\) 3. Project: \(\mathbf{m}_{k+1} = \text{DYKSTRA}(\tilde{\mathbf{m}})\)
End
  • Each model update depends only on the current model and gradient
  • Updated models satisfy all constraints after each iteration
  • Dykstra’s algorithm is parameter free in practice

Peters and Herrmann (2017), Algorithm 1.

Including constraints with projections

After including constraints, FWI solves (Esser et al. 2016):

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m}}\Phi(\mathbf{m})\quad \text{subject to} \quad\mathbf{m}+\Delta\mathbf{m}\,\in\, \mathcal{C} \]

At iteration \(k\), constraints are imposed by projecting:

\[ \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{m}^k) = \arg\min_\mathbf{m} \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{m}^k\|^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{m}\,\in\,\mathcal{C} \]

Properties:

  • When \(\mathcal{C}\) is convex, the projection is unique
  • Intersections of convex sets are also convex
  • Examples: box constraints (velocity bounds) and TV-norm ball (\(\text{TV}(\mathbf{m}) \leq \tau\))

The hinge loss — a constraint for salt

Key insight: In sedimentary basins, velocity generally increases with depth. Salt bodies are high-velocity inclusions — we can step into salt but should not easily step out.

The asymmetric (one-sided) TV constraint (Esser et al. 2016):

\[ \|\min(0, D_z \mathbf{m})\|_1 \leq \xi \]

  • \(D_z\) = vertical finite differences
  • Small \(\xi\): allow velocity to step into salt, discourage stepping out
  • Relaxing \(\xi\): gradually allows velocity lows to build up below salt

This is the hinge loss applied to vertical velocity differences — commonly used in machine learning for SVMs.

Multiple relaxed FWI cycles

  • Fixed box (\(v_{\min}=1500\), \(v_{\max}=5000\,\mathrm{m/s}\)) and TV (\(\tau=0.9\times \tau_{\text{true}}\))
  • Gradually relax hinge-loss \(\xi\) over warm-started FWI cycles
  • Salt body is remarkably well recovered

Adapted from (Esser et al. 2016). Snapshots show half of the continuation steps.

Why this automatic approach works

The constrained approach succeeds when:

  1. Relaxed constraints mitigate adverse effects of local minima from prior cycles
  2. Sharp reflectors of correct sign are introduced
  3. Progress is made during each FWI cycle
  4. “Fine-scales” of previous cycle contribute to “coarse-scales” of next cycle

Conceptual similarity to manual workflows:

Stage Manual Workflow Constrained FWI
1 Pick top salt TV + hinge-loss delineates top salt
2 Salt flood downward Hinge-loss allows buildup into salt
3 Pick bottom salt + tomography Relaxed hinge-loss allows velocity lows
4 Iterate Warm-started cycles

Part II: Constraints — Theory

Based on (Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann 2019)

Limitations of unconstrained regularization

Quadratic (Tikhonov) penalties (Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann 2019):

\[ \phi(\mathbf{m}) = f(\mathbf{m}) + \frac{\alpha_1}{2}\|R_1\mathbf{m}\|_2^2 + \frac{\alpha_2}{2}\|R_2\mathbf{m}\|_2^2 \]

Problems:

  • Penalties alter gradient and Hessian: \(\nabla^2_\mathbf{m} \phi = \nabla^2_\mathbf{m} f + \alpha_1 R_1^\top R_1 + \alpha_2 R_2^\top R_2\)
  • Trade-off parameters \(\alpha_i\) have no physical meaning and are interdependent with data misfit
  • Cannot directly encode non-smooth priors (TV norm, monotonicity)
  • Gradient filtering \(\mathbf{m}^{k+1} = \mathbf{m}^k - \gamma\, s(\nabla_\mathbf{m} f)\) — no theoretical guarantees

Convex sets — definition and properties

A set \(\mathcal{C}\) is convex if for all \(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(0 \leq c \leq 1\):

\[ c\mathbf{x} + (1-c)\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{C} \]

Key properties:

  • Intersection of convex sets is convex
  • Projection onto a convex set is unique
  • Projections are non-expansive: \(\|\mathcal{P}_\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathcal{P}_\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{y})\| \leq \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|\)

Advantages of the constrained formulation \(\min_\mathbf{m} f(\mathbf{m})\;\text{s.t.}\;\mathbf{m}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^p \mathcal{C}_i\):

  • Objective (data misfit) is unchanged — gradient/Hessian unaffected
  • Constraints in physical units (m/s, m/s per grid) — not abstract weights
  • Model iterates feasible at every iteration
  • Multiple constraints handled without trade-off parameters

Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann (2019)

POCS vs. Dykstra’s algorithm

To project onto the intersection of convex sets \(\mathcal{C} = \bigcap_{i=1}^p \mathcal{C}_i\):

POCS (Projection Onto Convex Sets): alternate projections \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_1}(\mathbf{m}))\)

  • Finds a feasible point — but NOT the closest one
  • Result is order-dependent (non-unique)

Dykstra’s algorithm (Dykstra 1983): alternate projections with auxiliary corrections

  • Finds the unique Euclidean projection onto the intersection
  • Result is order-independent
  • No tuning parameters

POCS vs. Dykstra — toy example

  • POCS: distances 1.16 and 1.14 (order-dependent)
  • Dykstra: distance 1.03 (unique minimum)

Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann (2019), Fig. 1.

Dykstra vs. POCS on Marmousi

  • Dykstra finds the boundary projection (closest to original)
  • POCS may find interior points far from the boundary — over-constraining the model

Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann (2019), Fig. 2.

Projected gradient descent

Update rule:

\[ \mathbf{m}^{k+1} = \mathcal{P}_\mathcal{C}\!\left(\mathbf{m}^k - \gamma\nabla_\mathbf{m} f(\mathbf{m}^k)\right) \]

  • Gradient step followed by projection onto \(\mathcal{C}\)
  • Constraints guide optimization to different (better) local minimum

Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann (2019), Fig. 4.

Spectral Projected Gradient (SPG)

Three key improvements over standard projected gradient (Birgin, Martı́nez, and Raydan 2000):

  1. Spectral stepsize \(\alpha\) — mimics Hessian via Barzilai-Borwein (Barzilai and Borwein 1988): \[\alpha = \frac{\mathbf{s}_k^\top \mathbf{s}_k}{\mathbf{s}_k^\top \mathbf{y}_k}\] where \(\mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{m}^{k+1} - \mathbf{m}^k\) and \(\mathbf{y}_k = \nabla f(\mathbf{m}^{k+1}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{m}^k)\)
  1. Non-monotone line search — allows temporary objective increase; reduces zigzagging
  1. Single projection per iteration — update direction \(\mathbf{p}^k = \mathcal{P}_\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{m}^k - \alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{m}^k)) - \mathbf{m}^k\); line search scales \(\gamma\) only

Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann (2019)

The 3-level nested optimization workflow

Level Method Role
Top SPG Nonlinear optimization (data misfit)
Middle Dykstra Project onto intersection of constraints
Bottom ADMM / closed-form Individual set projections

Each level is parameter-free by design.

SPG w/ multiple constraints — Algorithm

Algorithm 1: Spectral Projected Gradient w/ Dykstra (Peters, Smithyman, and Herrmann 2019).
Input: projectors \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_1}, \ldots, \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_p}\); initial \(\mathbf{m}^0 \in \bigcap \mathcal{C}_i\)
Initialize: \(M\) (line-search history), \(\eta \in (0,1)\), spectral step \(\alpha\), \(k=1\)
Repeat:
\(\quad\) 1. Compute \(f(\mathbf{m}^k)\) and \(\nabla_\mathbf{m} f(\mathbf{m}^k)\)
\(\quad\) 2. \(\mathbf{r}^k = \text{DYKSTRA}(\mathbf{m}^k - \alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{m}^k),\; \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_1}, \ldots, \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_p})\)
\(\quad\) 3. \(\mathbf{p}^k = \mathbf{r}^k - \mathbf{m}^k\)
\(\quad\) 4. Line search: find \(\gamma\) s.t. \(f(\mathbf{m}^k + \gamma\mathbf{p}^k) < \max(f_{k}, \ldots, f_{k-M}) + \epsilon\gamma\nabla f^\top \mathbf{p}^k\)
\(\quad\) 5. \(\mathbf{m}^{k+1} = \mathbf{m}^k + \gamma\mathbf{p}^k\)
\(\quad\) 6. Update spectral step: \(\alpha = \frac{\mathbf{s}_k^\top\mathbf{s}_k}{\mathbf{s}_k^\top\mathbf{y}_k}\)
Until convergence

Part III: Application to Salt

Based on (Esser et al. 2018) and (Esser et al. 2016)

Constrained WRI formulation

The full framework combines three constraints with the WRI objective (Esser et al. 2018):

\[ \min_m f(m) \quad \text{subject to} \quad m_i \in [b_i, B_i],\; \|m\|_{TV} \leq \tau,\; \|\max(0, D_z m)\|_1 \leq \xi \]

Constraint Role Parameter
Box Physical velocity bounds \(v_{\min}, v_{\max}\)
TV norm Edge preservation, denoising \(\tau\)
Hinge loss Discourage velocity decrease with depth \(\xi\)

Continuation strategy: gradually relax \(\xi\) over warm-started cycles while keeping box and TV fixed.

The hinge-loss constraint for salt

Asymmetric one-sided TV constraint in the vertical direction (Esser et al. 2018):

\[ \|\max(0, D_z m)\|_1 \leq \xi \]

Physical interpretation:

  • Penalizes the sum of positive vertical differences in slowness squared (= negative velocity jumps)
  • Small \(\xi\): velocity can step into high-velocity salt but cannot easily step out
  • Large \(\xi\): allows velocity lows to build up below salt

Continuation strategy:

  • Start with small \(\xi\) (nearly monotonic velocity increase)
  • Gradually increase \(\xi\) over successive warm-started passes
  • Sequence: \(\frac{\xi}{\xi_{\text{true}}} = .01,\, .05,\, .10,\, .15,\, .20,\, .25,\, .40,\, .90\)

In memoriam

John “Ernie” Esser\(^\dagger\) (May 19, 1980 – March 8, 2015)

The work presented in this lecture is in large part a tribute to Ernie Esser, who passed away in tragic circumstances. He was a young and very promising scientist whose contributions to constrained optimization for seismic inversion remain foundational.

To our knowledge, Ernie’s work is the first successful attempt to replace labour-intensive manual salt-flooding workflows with an automatic heuristic: successive relaxations of asymmetric TV-norm constraints with warm-started passes through frequency batches. (Esser et al. 2018)

Summary

  • Penalties vs. constraints: constraints are more predictable, require fewer parameters, and guarantee feasibility at every iterate
  • Key ingredients: box constraints (physical bounds), TV norm (edge preservation), hinge loss (asymmetric salt constraint)
  • Algorithms: Dykstra’s algorithm for projections onto intersections; SPG for nonlinear optimization; 3-level nested workflow
  • Results: automatic salt flooding from poor starting models on BP 2004 and SEAM benchmarks — replacing labor-intensive manual workflows
  • Both WRI and FWI benefit from the constrained framework
