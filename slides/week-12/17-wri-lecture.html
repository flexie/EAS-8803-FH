This is the second lecture in a series on Full-Waveform Inversion extensions. We build on the FWI foundations from the previous lecture and introduce Wavefield Reconstruction Inversion (WRI).

Recap: FWI as constrained optimization

FWI in the frequency domain is a PDE-constrained optimization problem:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}} \sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{1}{2}\|P_i\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{d}_i\|_2^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A_i(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}_i = \mathbf{q}_i \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{m}\) — gridded medium parameters (e.g., squared slowness)
  • \(A_i(\mathbf{m}) = \omega_i^2\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{m}) + L\) — discretized Helmholtz operator with \(L\) the discretized Laplacian
  • \(\mathbf{u}_i\) — wavefields, \(\mathbf{q}_i\) — sources
  • \(P_i\) — measurement (receiver sampling) operator
  • \(\mathbf{d}_i\) — observed \(i^{\text{th}}\) shot record

The Lagrangian (full-space) approach

An elegant way to solve the constrained optimization is via the Lagrangian (Haber, Ascher, and Oldenburg 2000):

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{1}{2}\|P_i\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{d}_i\|_2^2 + \mathbf{v}_i^{*}(A_i(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{q}_i) \]

where \(\mathbf{v} = [\mathbf{v}_1; \mathbf{v}_2; \ldots]\) are the Lagrange multipliers (adjoint wavefields).

Properties:

  • Dependency on \(\mathbf{m}\) is through \(A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}\) instead of \(A(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\mathbf{q}\) — less nonlinear
  • Explores a larger search space — less prone to local minima
  • No need to solve the PDE; constraints only satisfied upon convergence

The storage problem

Simultaneously updating and storing all \(2M\) wavefields (\(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)) is not feasible for large-scale problems.

The reduced formulation of FWI

Eliminating the constraints by solving the PDE gives the reduced objective:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m}} \phi_{\text{reduced}}(\mathbf{m}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{1}{2}\|P_iA_i(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{d}_i\|_2^2 \]

The gradient of the reduced objective is given by:

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\phi_{\text{reduced}}(\mathbf{m}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \omega_i^2\,\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{u}_i)^{*}\mathbf{v}_i \]

where the forward and adjoint wavefields must be computed:

\[ A_i(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}_i = \mathbf{q}_i, \qquad A_i(\mathbf{m})^{*}\mathbf{v}_i = P_i^{*}(P_i\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{d}_i) \]

Properties of the reduced formulation

Advantages:

  • Wavefields and gradient computed independently per source — parallelizable
  • No need to store all \(2M\) wavefields simultaneously

Disadvantages:

  • Computing the gradient requires solving both forward and adjoint wave equations for all \(M\) experiments at each iteration
  • The Hessian action requires three additional PDE solves per experiment (Pratt, Shin, and Hicks 1998)
  • The objective depends on \(\mathbf{m}\) through \(A(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\) — very nonlinear
  • This nonlinearity causes local minima (cycle skipping)

Can we come up with a formulation that combines advantages of both the Lagrangian and reduced formulations?

Motivation: the equation-error approach

Key insight (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016): If we had complete measurements of the wavefield (\(P\) invertible), we could:

  1. Reconstruct the state: \(\mathbf{u} = P^{-1}\mathbf{d}\)
  2. Recover \(\mathbf{m}\) by solving:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m}} \frac{1}{2}\|A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{q}\|_2^2 \]

which is linear in \(\mathbf{m}\) for many PDEs!

With incomplete measurements, we combine physics and data:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} A(\mathbf{m}) \\ P \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{u} \approx \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{q} \\ \mathbf{d} \end{pmatrix} \]

Part I: The Penalty Method

Leeuwen and Herrmann (2013)

The penalty formulation

To arrive at a method with the best of both worlds, cast the constrained problem into an unconstrained penalty form (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2013):

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}} \phi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \frac{1}{2}\|P_i\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{d}_i\|_2^2 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\|A_i(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{q}_i\|_2^2 \]

Two terms balanced by the trade-off parameter \(\lambda\):

Term Meaning
\(\frac{1}{2}\|P\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}\|_2^2\) Data misfit
\(\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\|A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{q}\|_2^2\) Wave-equation error
  • As \(\lambda \to \infty\): recovers the constrained (FWI) solution
  • For finite \(\lambda\): relaxes the physics — enlarges the search space over \(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}\)

The augmented wave equation

Minimizing \(\phi_\lambda\) w.r.t. \(\mathbf{u}\) (\(\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\phi_\lambda = 0\)) gives the augmented wave equation for each source:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \lambda A_i(\mathbf{m}) \\ P_i \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{u}_i = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \mathbf{q}_i \\ \mathbf{d}_i \end{pmatrix} \]

This overdetermined system is a regularized wave equation where the solution is forced to:

  • Obey the wave equation (weighted by \(\lambda\))
  • Fit the observed data at receiver locations

Wavefield Reconstruction

The solution reconstructs the wavefield from both the physics and the data — hence the name Wavefield Reconstruction Inversion (WRI).

The gradient and model update

The gradient of \(\phi_\lambda\) w.r.t. \(\mathbf{m}\) is:

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\phi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \lambda^2\omega_i^2\,\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{u}_i)^{*}\!\left(A_i(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{q}_i\right) \]

Compare to the reduced (FWI) gradient: \(\nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\phi_{\text{reduced}}(\mathbf{m}) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \omega_i^2\,\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{u}_i)^{*}\mathbf{v}_i\)

Key differences:

  • No adjoint wavefield \(\mathbf{v}_i\) needed — replaced by the PDE residual \((A_i\mathbf{u}_i - \mathbf{q}_i)\)
  • Correlates reconstructed wavefields with wave-equation residuals

Newton update for the model

Setting \(\nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\phi_{\lambda} = 0\) and using \(A_i(\mathbf{m}) = \omega_i^2\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{m}) + L\) gives the system:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{M} \omega_i^4\,\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{u}_i)^{*}\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{u}_i)\,\mathbf{m} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \omega_i^2\,\mathsf{diag}(\mathbf{u}_i)^{*}\!\left(\mathbf{q}_i - L\mathbf{u}_i\right) \]

Remarkable properties:

  • The system is strictly diagonal — trivial to solve
  • The left-hand side is a Hessian of \(\phi_\lambda\) w.r.t. \(\mathbf{m}\) (Newton step)
  • Can be assembled in a running sum without storing all wavefields
  • Independent computation and accumulation per source — parallelizable

Summary: penalty vs. reduced

The alternating approach has three major advantages over conventional FWI:

  1. No adjoint wavefields — updates computed directly from reconstructed wavefields and PDE residuals
  2. Each step is linear or mildly nonlinear — the augmented wave equation is linear in \(\mathbf{u}\); the model update is linear in \(\mathbf{m}\)
  3. Relaxes the physics — the wave equation is enforced in the least-squares sense, not exactly

Enlarged search space

The penalty objective depends on \(\mathbf{m}\) through the wave equation \(A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}\) (as in the Lagrangian), not through its inverse \(A(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\mathbf{q}\) (as in the reduced formulation). This arguably makes the problem less nonlinear.

Conclusions from Part I

The penalty formulation of wave-equation based inversion (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2013):

  1. Relaxes the physics by enforcing the wave equation in the least-squares sense rather than exactly
  2. Explores a larger search space by optimizing over both model and wavefields
  3. Leads to an efficient two-step algorithm: solve augmented wave equation, then update model via a diagonal system
  4. The method does not require adjoint wavefields — significant computational savings
  5. The trade-off parameter \(\lambda\) controls the basin of attraction — smaller \(\lambda\) widens it
  6. Initial tests show the method is less sensitive to inaccurate initial models
  7. Straightforward extensions to 3D, multi-parameter inversion, and randomized source subsampling

Part II: Theoretical Analysis

Leeuwen and Herrmann (2016)

Broader context: all-at-once methods

For linear PDEs, the inverse problem reads \[\min_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}} \frac{1}{2}\|P\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}\|_2^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{q}\]

The Lagrangian gradient and Hessian involve the Jacobian matrices (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016):

\[ G(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}) = \frac{\partial A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}}{\partial \mathbf{m}}, \quad K(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\partial A(\mathbf{m})^T\mathbf{v}}{\partial \mathbf{m}}, \quad R(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\partial G(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u})^T\mathbf{v}}{\partial \mathbf{m}} \]

The all-at-once approach finds a stationary point of \(\mathcal{L}\) by Newton iteration on the KKT system:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} R & K^T & G^T \\ K & P^TP & A^T \\ G & A & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \delta\mathbf{m} \\ \delta\mathbf{u} \\ \delta\mathbf{v} \end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} G^T\mathbf{v} \\ A^T\mathbf{v} + P^T(P\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{d}) \\ A\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{q} \end{pmatrix} \]

Eliminates the need to solve PDEs; constraints only satisfied upon convergence. But requires storing and updating all variables simultaneously — not feasible at scale.

The reduced Gauss-Newton algorithm

The reduced approach eliminates \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) via block elimination of the KKT system (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016):

Algorithm (Reduced Gauss-Newton):

  1. Initialize \(\mathbf{m}^0\)
  2. While \(\|\nabla_\mathbf{m}\mathcal{L}\|_2 \geq \epsilon\):
    1. Solve forward: \(\mathbf{u}_{\text{red}}^k = A(\mathbf{m}^k)^{-1}\mathbf{q}\)
    2. Solve adjoint: \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{red}}^k = A(\mathbf{m}^k)^{-T}P^T(\mathbf{d} - P\mathbf{u}_{\text{red}}^k)\)
    3. Gradient: \(\mathbf{g}^k = G(\mathbf{m}^k,\mathbf{u}_{\text{red}}^k)^T\mathbf{v}_{\text{red}}^k\)
    4. GN Hessian: \(H^k = G^TA^{-T}P^TPA^{-1}G\)
    5. Update: \(\mathbf{m}^{k+1} = \mathbf{m}^k - \alpha^k(H^k)^{-1}\mathbf{g}^k\)

Constraints satisfied at each iteration. Requires \(2K\) PDE solves per iteration (forward + adjoint).

The penalty objective and its reduction

Using a quadratic penalty, the constrained problem becomes (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016):

\[ \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}) = \frac{1}{2}\|P\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}\|_2^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{q}\|_2^2 \]

Eliminate \(\mathbf{u}\) by variable projection: \(\mathbf{u}_\lambda(\mathbf{m}) = \arg\min_{\mathbf{u}} \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u})\)

\[ \mathbf{u}_\lambda = \left(A^T\!A + \lambda^{-1}P^T\!P\right)^{-1}\!\left(A^T\mathbf{q} + \lambda^{-1}P^T\mathbf{d}\right) \]

This defines the reduced penalty objective:

\[ \phi_\lambda(\mathbf{m}) = \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}_\lambda(\mathbf{m})) \]

The modified system \(A^TA + \lambda^{-1}P^TP\) is a low-rank update of the original PDE — similar sparsity, similar conditioning.

The penalty Gauss-Newton algorithm

Algorithm (Penalty Gauss-Newton) (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016):

  1. Initialize \(\mathbf{m}^0\), choose \(\lambda\)
  2. While \(\|\nabla_\mathbf{m}\mathcal{L}\|_2 \geq \epsilon\):
    1. Solve augmented: \(\mathbf{u}_\lambda^k = (A^TA + \lambda^{-1}P^TP)^{-1}(A^T\mathbf{q} + \lambda^{-1}P^T\mathbf{d})\)
    2. Adjoint state: \(\mathbf{v}_\lambda^k = \lambda(A\mathbf{u}_\lambda^k - \mathbf{q})\)
    3. Gradient: \(\mathbf{g}_\lambda^k = G(\mathbf{m}^k,\mathbf{u}_\lambda^k)^T\mathbf{v}_\lambda^k\)
    4. GN Hessian: \(H_\lambda^k = \lambda G^T(I - A(A^TA + \lambda^{-1}P^TP)^{-1}A^T)G\)
    5. Update: \(\mathbf{m}^{k+1} = \mathbf{m}^k - \alpha^k(H_\lambda^k)^{-1}\mathbf{g}_\lambda^k\)

Key difference: The adjoint state \(\mathbf{v}_\lambda^k\) does not require an additional PDE solve — forward and adjoint are computed simultaneously via the normal equations.

Relationship to the reduced approach

Lemma (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016)

For fixed \(\mathbf{m}\), the penalty states converge to the reduced states:

\[ \mathbf{u}_\lambda = \mathbf{u}_{\text{red}} + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}), \qquad \mathbf{v}_\lambda = \mathbf{v}_{\text{red}} + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}) \]

with the natural scaling: \(\lambda > \|PA^{-1}\|_2^2\) is considered large.

Theorem 1 — Convergence (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016)

At each iteration: \(\|\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{u}\|_2 = 0\) and \(\|\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\).

If the algorithm terminates at \(\mathbf{m}^*\) with \(\|\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{m}\|_2 \leq \epsilon\), then:

\[ \|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{m}^*,\mathbf{u}^*_\lambda,\mathbf{v}^*_\lambda)\|_2 \leq \epsilon + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}) \]

Stationary point of the Lagrangian within finite accuracy with finite \(\lambda\).

Distance to the true solution

Theorem 2 — Error bound (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016)

\[ \|\mathbf{m}_\lambda^* - \mathbf{m}^*\|_2 \leq \kappa(H)\left(\tilde{\epsilon} + \tilde{\lambda}^{-1}\tilde{\eta}\right) \]

where:

Symbol Meaning
\(\kappa(H)\) Condition number of reduced GN Hessian
\(\tilde{\epsilon} = \epsilon/\|H\|_2\) Scaled tolerance
\(\tilde{\lambda} = \lambda/\|PA^{-1}\|_2^2\) Scaled penalty parameter
\(\tilde{\eta} = \|\mathbf{d} - PA^{-1}\mathbf{q}\|_2/\sqrt{\|H\|_2}\) Scaled data error

Implication: For ill-posed inverse problems, we only reconstruct dominant eigenmodes of \(H\) — a finite \(\lambda\) suffices for an acceptable reconstruction.

Computational complexity

The penalty method achieves similar per-iteration cost to the reduced method while exploring a larger search space. All PDE solves can be done independently and in parallel.

Algorithm details

Solving the augmented PDE (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016):

  • Factorization: \(P^TP\) is nearly diagonal \(\Rightarrow\) \(A^TA + \lambda^{-1}P^TP\) has similar sparsity as \(A^TA\)
  • Iterative methods: LSQR, LSMR, or CGLS applicable. Eigenvalue perturbation is bounded: condition number does not worsen
  • Direct methods: For time-stepping with lower-triangular \(A\), the augmented system becomes banded — solvable via block Thomas algorithm

Choosing \(\lambda\):

  • Natural scaling: \(\lambda = \tilde{\lambda}\cdot\mu_1(A^{-T}P^TPA^{-1})\)
  • Monitor \(\lambda^{-1}\|\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{v}\|_2\) relative to \(\|\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{m}\|_2\) during iterations
  • Increase \(\lambda\) when the penalty method stalls
  • A single fixed \(\lambda\) is typically sufficient

Nonlinearity comparison

Misfit surface \(\phi(\mathbf{m}^* + a_1\mathbf{v}_1 + a_2\mathbf{v}_2)\) plotted along slowly oscillatory perturbations. 2D ultrasound tomography at 5 kHz (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016).

Nonlinearity: reduced vs. penalty

The reduced objective shows strong nonlinearity and spurious stationary points. For small \(\lambda\), the penalty misfit is much better behaved. As \(\lambda\) increases, the penalty misfit converges to the reduced misfit (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016).

2D ultrasound: ground truth and convergence

Gauss-Newton method. The gradient stalls at \(\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\) as predicted. Continuation in \(\lambda = \{0.1,1,10,100,1000\}\) reaches the desired tolerance. Penalty method converges in fewer iterations and uses fewer PDE solves (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016).

2D ultrasound: reconstructions

Parameter estimates are nearly identical across methods. All methods start overfitting after a few iterations (countered by regularization or early stopping). The penalty method gives similar results at lower computational cost (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016).

Part III: WRI* — Time-Domain WRI

Rizzuti et al. (2021)

The computational bottleneck of WRI

Classical WRI solves the augmented wave equation:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} R \\ \lambda A(\mathbf{m}) \end{pmatrix} \bar{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{m}) = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{d} \\ \lambda \mathbf{q} \end{pmatrix} \]

or equivalently:

\[ (R^*R + \lambda^2 A^*A)\bar{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{m}) = R^*\mathbf{d} + \lambda^2 A^*\mathbf{q} \]

Problem

This augmented system cannot be trivially solved in the time domain using standard time-marching schemes. WRI is practically limited to 2D frequency-domain problems!

The denoising reformulation

Rizzuti et al. (2021) propose to swap objective and constraint:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{q} - A(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \|\mathbf{d} - R\mathbf{u}\| \leq \epsilon \]

Instead of penalizing the wave-equation error, we:

  • Minimize the wave-equation error
  • Constrain the data misfit to be within a noise level \(\epsilon\)

This is equivalent to the penalty formulation for a suitable \(\lambda(\epsilon)\) — but leads to a lower-dimensional Lagrangian multiplier (data space instead of wavefield space).

The WRI* Lagrangian

Using Fenchel duality (Rockafellar 1970) and variable projection \(\bar{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{m}) = \arg\min_\mathbf{u} \mathcal{L}_\text{dat}\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{WRI}^*}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{y}) = -\frac{1}{2}\|F^*\mathbf{y}\|^2 + \langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m})\rangle - \epsilon\|\mathbf{y}\| \]

where:

  • \(F(\mathbf{m}) = RA(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\) — the forward modeling operator
  • \(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}) = \mathbf{d} - F(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{q}\) — the data residual
  • \(\mathbf{y}\) — Lagrangian multipliers in data space

Key advantage

Evaluating \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{WRI}^*}\) and its gradients only requires solutions of the conventional wave equation — enabling time-domain solvers!

Gradient computation for WRI*

The gradients are (Rizzuti et al. 2021):

\[ \nabla_\mathbf{m}\mathcal{L}_{\text{WRI}^*} = -J[\mathbf{m},\mathbf{q}+F^*\mathbf{y}]^*\mathbf{y} \]

\[ \nabla_\mathbf{y}\mathcal{L}_{\text{WRI}^*} = \mathbf{d} - F(\mathbf{q}+F^*\mathbf{y}) - \epsilon\frac{\mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{y}\|} \]

where \(J[\mathbf{m},\mathbf{f}]\Delta\mathbf{m} = \partial_\mathbf{m}(F\mathbf{f})\Delta\mathbf{m}\) is the Jacobian action.

The model gradient is computed similarly to FWI:

  1. Backpropagate \(\mathbf{y}\) to form extended source \(\bar{\mathbf{q}} = A^{-*}R^*\mathbf{y}\)
  2. Forward propagate \(\bar{\mathbf{u}} = A^{-1}(\mathbf{q} + \bar{\mathbf{q}})\)
  3. Cross-correlate \(\bar{\mathbf{q}}\) and \(\partial_{tt}\bar{\mathbf{u}}\)

Dual variable approximation

Solving for the optimal \(\bar{\mathbf{y}}\) exactly is expensive. Instead, use the cheap approximation (Rizzuti et al. 2021):

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \tilde{\alpha}(\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}), \qquad \tilde{\alpha} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\|\mathbf{r}\|(\|\mathbf{r}\|-\epsilon)}{\|F^*\mathbf{r}\|^2}, & \|\mathbf{r}\| \geq \epsilon \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

  • Obtained from variable projection: \(\tilde{\alpha} = \arg\max_\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{WRI}^*}(\mathbf{m},\alpha\mathbf{r})\)
  • No extra cost — computed as byproduct of objective evaluation
  • The corrected gradient (accounting for \(\tilde{\alpha}\) dependence on \(\mathbf{m}\)) improves depth scaling

WRI* algorithm

Source-dependent weighting

To enhance robustness, apply a source-dependent metric to the wave equation error (Huang, Nammour, and Symes 2018; Symes 2020):

\[ \mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_s|^2 + h^2}/h \]

This penalizes extended source energy located far from the physical source position.

  • Crucial for avoiding local minima in WRI and WRI* (Symes 2020)
  • \(h\) tuned to a fraction of the reference wavelength
  • Cannot be trivially incorporated in FWI (based on exact wave-equation solutions)
  • Enhances resolution for model portions far from source

Including the gradient correction visibly improves depth scaling and better approximates the optimal WRI gradient. Frequency domain, 5 Hz, Marmousi model (Rizzuti et al. 2021).

Constraint Enforced exactly Relaxed via \(\lambda\) Relaxed via \(\epsilon\)
Domain Time or frequency Frequency (2D) Time domain (3D)
PDE solves \(2K\) per iter \(K\) per iter \(4K\) per iter
Local minima Severe Mitigated Mitigated

Wrong physics Sensitive More robust More robust

Key takeaways

  1. WRI relaxes the physics by not solving the wave equation exactly — enlarging the search space and reducing nonlinearity (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2013)

  2. The penalty method finds stationary points of the Lagrangian within finite precision using a finite \(\lambda\) — no need for \(\lambda \to \infty\) (Leeuwen and Herrmann 2016)

  3. WRI* reformulates WRI via Fenchel duality, enabling time-domain solvers at roughly 2\(\times\) the cost of FWI — making it feasible for 3D (Rizzuti et al. 2021)

  4. WRI/WRI* are more robust to cycle skipping and inaccurate modeling assumptions (e.g., wrong anisotropy)

  5. Source weighting is essential for WRI/WRI* to avoid local minima (Symes 2020)

  6. Hybrid WRI*/FWI strategies can recover resolution while maintaining robustness
